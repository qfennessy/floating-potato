Venus Balloon Navigator

Race balloons around Venus against AI pilots. The only way to steer is to go up or down.

Watch the race Fly it yourself

Balloons can't steer. But they can pick their wind.

The ground on Venus is hot enough to melt lead. Landers break within hours. But high up in the clouds, 50 to 62 km above the ground, the air is about as warm as Earth. A balloon can float there. Two did, in 1985.

Up there, the wind never stops. It carries everything west, all the way around the planet every 4 or 5 days. A balloon can't push against it. It can only go up or down. Each height has its own wind:

  • Higher up, the wind is faster and pushes you toward the poles.
  • Lower down, it's slower and pushes you toward the equator.
  • In between, the push changes direction.

So picking a height picks how fast you go and which way you drift. That's the only steering you get.

The goal: score as many points as you can in 30 days

Famous places

60 real mountains, plains and highlands, like Maat Mons. Fly within 350 km of one for 1 point.

Eruptions

70 real volcanoes go off now and then, often several times in a row. Fly past an eruption (2 points), a gas cloud (3) or lightning (1) before it fades.

Messages from Earth

Four times a flight, mission control sends a note: a bonus place to visit, a warning that a volcano will erupt, and so on. Only pilots who read them can act on them.

Risky samples

Air samples from very high up or very low down, 3 points each. Worth it only if your balloon survives the trip.

What can go wrong

Limited up and down

Going up uses ballast. Going down lets out gas. Each balloon only has enough for so many moves. Run out, and you're stuck at your height.

Wear and tear

Fly too high and acid eats the balloon. Fly too low and the heat does. Stay too long and you can lose it.

The finish line

Points stop at day 30. A balloon that's still fine on day 31 wasted its chances. The smart move: play safe early, take risks near the end.

The pilots

  • Gemini Robotics-ER 2, an AI from Google, looks at a map every 2 days and decides where to go next: stay at one latitude, wander after new places, take risks, or head for a target. It says why in one sentence.
  • Gemini 3.8 Flash, a faster Google AI, gets exactly the same map and choices.
  • The rulebook is a few simple rules, no AI: stay where there's most to see, move on when it runs out, and take risks in the last 6 days.
  • Rulebook + Gemini lets the rules do the flying, and only asks the AI when something unusual happens, like a message from Earth.
  • Fixed balloons pick one height and never change it, like the real 1985 balloons. They show what you get with no steering at all.
  • You fly your own balloon with a slider, racing everyone else.

Between decisions, the same autopilot does the actual flying for every pilot, so it's a fair fight. Each race happens in a different "world" with its own starting point and weather, and the pilots only get a forecast, not the real winds.

Who wins? 20 races, 30 days each

When there's danger, judgment pays. Gemini ER 2 picked its moments to take risks and lost just 1 balloon in 20. The rulebook took risks on a fixed schedule and lost 5. The rulebook still visited the most places.

How to watch

The globe

Moving dots show the wind: blue drifts north, orange drifts south. Heights are stretched 30 times so you can see them. A red flash is an eruption, and the ring around it shrinks as time runs out.

The panels

The wind panel shows how fast each height blows and which way it pushes. One line says what the pilot you're following is doing. The bar under each score shows how worn its balloon is.

The controls

Click a score at the top to follow that balloon. "Director" pauses to show the best moments. Space bar pauses. Up and down arrows fly your balloon.
