Venus Balloon Navigator

AI pilots, a simple rulebook and you race balloons around Venus, steering only by choosing which altitude's wind to ride.

Watch the race Fly it yourself

Balloons can't steer. But they can pick their wind.

Venus's surface is about 465°C, and landers die within hours. But 50 to 62 km up, inside the clouds, the air is close to Earth's temperature and pressure. Balloons can live there: the Soviet-French Vega balloons floated at 54 km for two days in 1985.

The whole cloud layer races around the planet, once every 4 or 5 days. A balloon can't push sideways, but it can go up or down, and each height has a different wind:

  • Higher is faster, and drifts toward the poles.
  • Lower is slower, and drifts toward the equator.
  • Somewhere in the middle, the north-south drift flips.

So choosing a height chooses both your speed and your north-south drift. That's the only steering there is.

The mission: 30 days, as many points as possible

60 named sites

Real features from the USGS/IAU gazetteer, like Maat Mons and Beta Regio. 1 point each, the first time you pass within 350 km.

Eruptions and plumes

70 real volcanoes erupt now and then, and they erupt in spells. Catch an eruption (2), a gas plume (3), or lightning (1) before it fades.

Mission control

Four plain-text messages per flight: plumes worth triple, a bonus site, a relay outage, a volcano alert. Only pilots that read them can act on them.

Risky science

Cloud-top samples above 63 km and deep-air samples below 48 km, 3 points each. Worth it only if you can survive getting them.

Risk and resources

Ballast and gas

Climbing drops ballast, sinking vents gas: 45 km of each for the whole flight. Run out and that direction is gone.

Wear and loss

Outside 50–62 km, acid above and heat below wear out the envelope, and every hour there risks losing the balloon.

The finish line

Scoring stops at day 30. Surviving past it is worth nothing, so the smart move is to take risks late, not early, and finish with nothing left.

The pilots

  • Gemini Robotics-ER 2 looks at a map every 2 days and picks a lane, roams, sprints into the risky layers, or heads for a place, and explains why in one sentence.
  • Gemini 3.8 Flash gets exactly the same map, prompt and tools.
  • The rulebook is plain code: hold the busiest lane until it runs dry, then roam, and sprint in the last 6 days.
  • Rulebook + Gemini lets the rules fly and asks Gemini only when something comes up: a message, an open request, a burst of eruptions.
  • Fixed balloons never change height, like Vega in 1985. The best single height across all worlds is the fair rival.
  • You fly live with a slider, against everyone's saved flights.

Between decisions, the same autopilot flies every pilot's balloon: a search planner that weighs risk against the days left, and a lane holder that learns the real winds. Each world has its own launch point and weather; the pilots only know a forecast.

Once risk enters the picture, judgment pays: Gemini ER 2 chose when to dive into the dangerous layers and lost one balloon in 20. The rulebook dives on a fixed schedule and lost five. The rulebook still sweeps the most sites.

How to watch

The globe

Amber particles are the wind near the selected balloon, blue for northward, orange for southward. Height is exaggerated about 30×. A crimson pulse is an eruption; its halo shrinks as time runs out.

The panels

The wind panel lists each layer's speed and push. One sentence says what the selected pilot is doing. Score chips drain as balloons wear.

The controls

Click a score chip to follow a balloon. The Director pauses and frames each eruption catch. Space pauses. ↑ ↓ fly your own balloon.
